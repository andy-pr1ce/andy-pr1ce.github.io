Rebuilding under pressure: eight years in Arizona

When the council lost its largest chartered partner, it had to stabilize first and then rethink everything. The windshield, not the rearview mirror, guided the work.

The situation

When I arrived in late 2018, the council was absorbing the Latter-day Saint Church's decision to end its Scouting-based youth program, which meant losing an extraordinary share of membership and revenue. The challenge ran deeper: $1.2 million in operating debt, a fragile cash position, relationships with many other units that had gone untended, and a board and staff model built for a different era.

Then came COVID and Chapter 11.

"Things were so broken that we were willing to break what remained and rebuild a council operation for the 21st century."

Stabilize first

With the board, we drew $2.2 million from the endowment to retire the operating debt and create $1 million in working capital. We restructured, cutting personnel expense 42% ($1.36 million a year) by 2020. We outsourced accounting, strengthened controls, and resolved the causes of two inherited qualified audits. We sold underperforming properties, and the sale of Little Grand Canyon Ranch alone produced $1.5 million toward our settlement contribution.

Then rebuild

Stabilization was never the strategy. We used the crisis to rethink the operating model: governance and board capability, staffing and field deployment, volunteer training, compensation, fundraising, property strategy, and marketing built on research with ASU. When school access limited recruiting, we moved past a passive public-policy posture and helped build support for the statewide school-access legislation that passed in 2026.

Permission to be imperfect

The next model doesn't need to be perfect before you start moving toward it. When the status quo is clearly inadequate, waiting for certainty can be the riskiest choice. We piloted a dedicated Unit Growth Executive role. When it didn't outperform our broader field model, we ended it and reallocated the capacity. Commitment to the outcome has to be stronger than attachment to any one idea.

What I'd carry forward

  • Face the facts early.
  • Distinguish mission from tradition.
  • Stabilize what must be stabilized, then rebuild rather than shrink.
  • Use evidence rather than nostalgia.
  • Give capable people permission to experiment, and stop what doesn't work.
