Strengthening movements that matter

Andy Price · November 2026 · 3 min read

Societies rise or fall based on the character and capability of their people. Communities need leaders who can build trust, solve difficult problems, work across differences, and serve something greater than themselves. Those qualities are developed intentionally, not by accident.

That is why the mission matters.

Organizations exist to advance a mission, not to preserve themselves. The mission is larger than any individual leader and more enduring than any organizational structure. Organizations are simply vehicles through which missions are accomplished, and leaders are entrusted with ensuring those organizations remain capable of fulfilling their purpose.

Scouting has long described itself as a movement rather than simply an organization, and that distinction is important. Organizations coordinate people; movements inspire them. The movement exists because the mission matters, and the organization exists to support the movement. Every strategic decision should ultimately answer one question: Will this better enable us to fulfill our mission?

Over the course of my career, my understanding of leadership has evolved. Early on, I admired leaders whose personalities defined their organizations. Experience taught me that the strongest leaders build organizations that become less dependent on them over time. They strengthen people, systems, and culture so the mission continues to thrive long after they have moved on.

One principle has quietly guided my career: everything entrusted to me should be better when I leave than when I arrived. Whether leading an organization, developing a new initiative, or mentoring future leaders, my responsibility is to leave it stronger than I found it.

Leadership is temporary. Stewardship is enduring.

Every leader inherits an organization shaped by those who came before and has an obligation to prepare it for those who will follow. That requires preserving what continues to serve the mission while having the courage to improve what no longer does. Loyalty to the mission should never be confused with loyalty to existing structures or familiar traditions. The mission is timeless; the methods must continually evolve.

That philosophy shapes how I lead. Strategy provides direction. Accountability turns priorities into results. Trust creates the foundation for healthy teams. Engagement brings people together around a common purpose. My role is not to be the center of the organization, but to create the conditions in which people, teams, and the mission can flourish.

Success is ultimately measured by what remains after the leader is gone. If the mission is reaching more people, the organization is healthier, future leaders inherit stronger systems, and communities are better served because I had the opportunity to lead, then I will consider my career a success.
